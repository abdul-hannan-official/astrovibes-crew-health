Connected Devices & Data Flow

How each reading reaches this app in a real mission. Devices measure; the app decides. Vitals come from sensors automatically — manual typing is only a fallback when a device is unavailable, plus the few things only a person can report (mood, symptoms, sleep quality, vision). Every manually entered value is tagged MANUAL in the CSV export, so a flight surgeon always knows where a number came from.

Demo note: this build runs on a simulated device feed — each card below shows the real device it stands in for, and in a live deployment it would show live status, battery and last sync instead of “simulated feed”.

The devices

Astroskin Bio-Monitor — smart shirt

Role: wearable, worn in the cabin, 24/7. Measures: heart rate, 3-lead ECG, SpO2, systolic blood-pressure estimate, skin temperature, breathing rate and volume, activity, sleep. Connection: Bluetooth LE to the on-board hub. Status: connected (simulated feed). Real world: Canadian Space Agency technology, flown on the ISS.

Tempus Pro — medical station

Role: on-demand and daily checks, used by the crew medic or the astronaut themself. Measures: ECG, non-invasive blood pressure (cuff), SpO2, temperature, plus ultrasound, laryngoscope and camera. Built-in iAssist guides each step. Connection: USB / spacecraft LAN. Status: connected (simulated feed). Real world: tested by NASA in the HESTIA chamber (2023) and flying on ISS missions.

Wrist Actigraph — Standard Measures

Role: wearable, 24/7. Measures: motion, sleep and light exposure — the Artemis II Standard Measures set for deep space. Connection: Bluetooth LE. Status: connected (simulated feed).

Personal Radiation Dosimeter

Role: worn at all times. Measures: cumulative radiation dose (mSv) and dose rate. Connection: spacecraft LAN, auto-sync every hour. Status: connected (simulated feed). Why it matters: radiation is one of NASA’s 5 hazards; dose limits come from NASA-STD-3001.

Cabin Environment Sensors

Role: fixed in the spacecraft. Measures: cabin temperature, pressure, CO2, noise and ambient radiation. Connection: spacecraft LAN. Status: connected (simulated feed).

EVA Suit Sensors — spacewalks only

Role: worn inside the suit during a spacewalk. Measures: heart rate, suit temperature, oxygen and CO2 levels, suit pressure. Connection: suit telemetry to the spacecraft. Status: idle on non-EVA days — the suit is not worn in the cabin, so daily health comes from the shirt and Tempus Pro, not the suit.

What is manual, and what is never manual

From devices (automatic)

Heart rate, ECG, SpO2, blood pressure, temperature, breathing, activity, sleep hours, radiation dose, cabin environment. If a device stops sending, the app says “no data” — it never invents a value.

From the person (manual, by design)

Mood, stress, symptoms, sleep quality and vision — only the astronaut can feel and report these. Manual entry is also the fallback if a device is down, and every such value is tagged MANUAL.

The 5 Hazards of Human Spaceflight

Source: nasa.gov/hrp/hazards/ — hazards do not stand alone; they feed off one another and worsen effects on the body.

1. Space Radiation

Invisible to the human eye, and considered one of the most hazardous aspects of spaceflight.

2. Isolation and Confinement

Behavioural responses occur in groups isolated and confined in a small space over a long period of time.

3. Distance from Earth

Instructions, supplies and medical care become harder to receive. Mars is on average 140 million miles away — a roughly 3-year trip, versus a 3-day lunar trip. Self-sufficiency is key.

4. Gravity Fields

Muscles, bones, inner ear and organs must adjust. On Mars, astronauts live in three-eighths of Earth’s gravity for up to two years.

5. Hostile / Closed Environments

Temperature, pressure, lighting, noise, microbes and immune responses inside the spacecraft must be kept safe.

Ongoing HRP Research — studies this project refers to

Source: nasa.gov/hrp/ongoing-hrp-research/

Tempus Pro

A commercial telemedicine device that collects and integrates health data, potentially enabling remote, near real-time diagnosis. The closest real-world model for this challenge.

Standard Measures

A consistent set of physiological and psychological measurements, collected the same way so future researchers can learn how spaceflight affects crew.

CIPHER

A suite of studies examining multiple body systems to understand how the human body changes while orbiting Earth.

ARCHeR (Artemis II)

Monitors astronauts’ well-being, activity and sleep patterns to learn about human health and performance in deep space.

Immune Biomarkers (Artemis II)

Blood and saliva samples showing how deep space changes the immune system.

Thigh Cuff & Brain Fluid Pressure

Testing whether thigh cuffs keep fluids in the legs to avert eye disorders, and how fluid pressure inside the skull relates to eye and brain changes.

More NASA data sources — open data this project refers to

These are real, public NASA sources. They are used as references only — their data is not fed into this demo’s risk scores, which use labelled simulated readings.

NASA Open Science Data Repository (OSDR)

Multi-modal space life-science data: GeneLab omics plus ALSDA physiology, behaviour and environmental telemetry, with a RadLab radiation-telemetry API. science.nasa.gov — About OSDR

NASA-STD-3001 — Crew Health Standard

The Agency-level Space Flight Human-System Standard. Volume 1 (Crew Health) sets the requirements, thresholds and limits for astronaut health and medical care; Volume 2 covers human factors and habitability. nasa.gov — the standard, explained

Ames Life Sciences Data Archive (ALSDA)

NASA’s official repository for non-human life-sciences data from the Space Biology and Human Research Programs — now part of OSDR. The model for keeping research data separate from live crew data.

Canadian Space Agency — Radiation Monitoring

How Canadian scientists monitor space radiation to protect astronaut health — the radiation context behind this app’s cumulative-dose tracking. asc-csa.gc.ca

Privacy & safety

  • Health data is sensitive. In this demo it is stored only in this browser (localStorage) and never sent to a server.
  • Roles limit what is shown: an Astronaut sees only their own record; a Flight Surgeon sees the whole crew.
  • This software flags risk using stated rules and thresholds. It does not diagnose disease, prescribe treatment, or decide mission readiness. Always follow the flight surgeon / medical team.
  • All readings are simulated demonstration data. Thresholds in this app are demonstration thresholds, not medically validated flight standards.
  • Not a NASA product. Not endorsed by NASA. Not a medical device.
